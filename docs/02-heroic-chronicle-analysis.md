# Heroic Chronicle — Gameplay Analysis

An assessment of the Heroic Chronicle (EGW, Chapter 4) as a tool at the table. It covers what it does well, where it breaks down and what that means for our rework. Rules details are in [01-heroic-chronicle-reference.md](01-heroic-chronicle-reference.md). This document assumes you've read it or have it open.

## 1. The short version

The Heroic Chronicle is **an excellent story generator attached to a poorly built reward system.**

The narrative half works. It drops a character into a specific place, hands them a web of people who care about them (for better or worse), and gives the DM ready-made plot hooks. The Prophecy system is the best idea in the chapter: player-written goals with built-in complications, plus real pacing guidance.

The mechanical half is the problem. Almost all the power comes from **fateful moments**, and those are:

- **Rare.** About **75%** of fully rolled characters get none.
- **Wildly uneven.** One d20 result gives a single skill proficiency. Another gives lycanthropy and immunity to most weapon damage.
- **Disconnected from the story.** You get one because an ally or rival happened to roll a scary stat block, not because of anything in your past.

So in most parties, some players walk away with a feat or a magic item and others get nothing. That gap is built into the dice.

| Area | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Setting integration | ✅ Strong |
| NPC web (allies, rivals, family) | ✅ Strong concept, ⚠️ uneven execution |
| Plot hooks (Secrets, Prophecy) | ✅ Strong |
| Player agency (roll *or* choose) | ✅ Strong |
| Mechanical rewards (fateful moments) | ❌ Weak: rare, unbalanced, arbitrary trigger |
| Prophecy rewards | ⚠️ Too small, and they don't scale |
| Structural completeness | ⚠️ Gaps (no class step, missing table columns, edge cases) |
| Time cost | ⚠️ Heavy, but acceptable for a session zero |

## 2. How we judged it

We looked at the system from both sides of the screen and asked six questions:

| Lens | Question |
|---|---|
| **Inspiration** | Does it give players ideas they wouldn't have had alone? |
| **Table use** | Does it give the DM material they'll actually use in play? |
| **Fairness** | Do characters in the same party end up roughly equal in power? |
| **Coherence** | Do the results fit together into one believable life? |
| **Agency** | Can players steer it toward the character they want? |
| **Cost** | How long does it take, and is that time well spent? |

Where we quote probabilities, they assume **every step is rolled and nothing is chosen**. Section 6 has the full method. Real tables mix rolling and choosing, but the all-rolled case shows what the designers' dice actually produce.

## 3. Strengths

### 3.1 It makes the setting personal

Every character comes out with a region, a government, a specific settlement, a social standing in that nation, and even a favourite dish. That's a lot of concrete grounding for a setting that new players won't know.

The **Social Status** step is especially smart. The same background reads differently depending on where you grew up. A Folk Hero is admired in the Clovis Concord but seen as a troublemaker in the Dwendalian Empire. Teaching the setting's politics *through the character's own life* is far better than a lore dump.

> **Keep:** setting knowledge delivered through the character's own situation, not as exposition.

### 3.2 It builds an NPC web for the DM

An average character comes out with about **three named relationships**: 2 family members on average from the d3, plus about 0.9 acquired allies or rivals. Each has a reason to care about the PC. Across a party of five, that's roughly 15 NPCs, each already tied to a player.

The relationship tables are good at this. Many entries include a **built-in behaviour rule** the DM can run with:

- *"They'll help, but not at risk of injury or death."* (Family 61–70)
- *"They come to you every time they're in trouble."* (Rival 81–90)
- *"Their help always makes things worse."* (Rival 91–00)

These tell the DM how the NPC acts, not just who they are. That's the most useful thing an NPC hook can do.

The rival table also ranges from deadly (*"want your blood"*) to nuisance (*"want to be your friend"*). Not every rival has to be a villain, which keeps the web from becoming nothing but enemies.

> **Keep:** relationships that come with a behaviour rule. Keep the range of rival intensity.

### 3.3 Mysterious Secrets are ideal plot seeds

The d20 Secrets table is the most consistently strong table in the chapter. Every entry is:

- **Concrete:** a glowing blue key, a ruby-centred coin, a falling star that smiles.
- **Unexplained:** the player knows *what* happened but not *why*.
- **DM-owned:** the DM decides what it means, so it can be tied into the campaign later.

That's exactly the shape a plot hook should have. It also feeds Prophecy, since the book suggests linking a goal to the secret.

> **Keep:** the "concrete, unexplained, DM-owned" pattern. It works well as a template for other tables.

### 3.4 Prophecy turns backstory into forward momentum

Most backstory tools only look backwards. Prophecy asks the player **what their character wants next**, at three timescales (immediate, long-term, arc-ending), and makes them write a **complication** for each.

That gives the system several strengths:

- **Player-authored.** It's the player's story, so they're invested in it.
- **Built-in twist.** The complication stops a goal from simply resolving and keeps the character's story moving.
- **Staggered.** Only the immediate goal is needed on day one, so players aren't asked to plan an arc before they know the campaign.
- **Real pacing guidance.** *Sessions per goal ≈ campaign length ÷ total goals* is rare in D&D books: an actual number a DM can plan around.
- **Spotlight philosophy.** The book says hitting goals more often than planned is fine, because players would rather see their own story come up. That's a sound read of what players enjoy.

> **Keep:** the three-tier goal structure, the complications, and the pacing formula. This is the core worth building on.

### 3.5 Rolling and choosing are both first-class options

The book repeatedly says you can choose any result, reroll a bad fit, or let a roll change your concept. That flexibility serves both kinds of player: the one who wants a surprise and the one who arrives with a finished concept.

The **secret identity** option, where the DM rolls an ally's identity in private, is a nice touch. It lets the dice create mysteries the player discovers in play.

> **Keep:** "roll or choose" at every step, and the option to keep some rolls hidden.

### 3.6 It also works for villains

The book points out that DMs can use it for villains. Running a major NPC through the same process gives them family, a home, rivals and a secret. That makes them feel like they belong to the same world as the PCs.

## 4. Weaknesses

### 4.1 The fateful moment trigger is arbitrary

Fateful moments are where nearly all the mechanical power lives. You get one only when an **acquired** ally or rival rolls a ★ identity (Mage, Noble, Assassin, dragon, etc.).

Problems with that:

- **The trigger has nothing to do with the moment.** Your rival happens to be an Assassin, so you get a d20 roll that might say you *spent a year with a travelling circus*. The rival and the event aren't connected.
- **Family never counts.** Family relationships don't roll on the Identities table, so a mother who would do anything for you can't produce one. Only acquired relationships can.
- **It's gated behind background × nation.** Your *number* of acquired relationships comes from the Social Status table. An **Outlander has a 95% chance** of getting none at all, so they can almost never earn a fateful moment. Overall, **19%** of rolled characters have no acquired relationships and so no chance of one.

The result is that the most rewarding part of the system depends on three unrelated rolls in a row, none of which the player experiences as a story.

### 4.2 Most characters get nothing, so parties end up uneven

With everything rolled:

| Fateful moments | Share of characters |
|---|---|
| 0 | **≈ 75%** |
| 1 | ≈ 24% |
| 2 | ≈ 1% |

That's for one character. The real issue is the **party**:

| Party size | Nobody gets one | **Some do, some don't** | Everyone gets one |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 players | 31% | **69%** | 0.4% |
| 5 players | 23% | **77%** | 0.1% |

So in a typical group, the system **creates a power gap at level 1** about 70–77% of the time. One player has Magic Initiate or a pseudodragon, and the person next to them has nothing. A uniform boost would at least be fair. Rewarding a lucky minority isn't.

### 4.3 The fateful moments themselves are badly unbalanced

Even among characters who get one, the d20 is a lottery. Grouping the results by rough power:

| Tier | d20 results | Share | What you get |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Campaign-altering** | 13, 14 | 10% | **Lycanthropy.** Under MM rules a PC lycanthrope gains the creature's damage immunities (nonmagical, non-silvered weapons) and traits. At level 1 that's close to invulnerability against ordinary enemies. |
| **Feat or permanent magic** | 2, 3, 5, 7, 9, 12, 15, 16 | 40% | A feat (Martial Adept, Magic Initiate), an uncommon magic item, *speak with animals* at will, a **quasit familiar** (invisible scout), or a pseudodragon companion |
| **Solid proficiencies** | 1, 8, 10, 11, 17, 18 | 30% | Two proficiencies or a proficiency bundle, sometimes with a tool, a horse, or a madness as the price |
| **Minor** | 4, 6, 19, 20 | 20% | One skill, a treasure map (only as good as the DM makes it), or 100 gp and a ring |

Specific pain points:

- **#10 (press-ganged)** gives medium armour, shields and martial weapons. For a wizard or sorcerer that's a big defensive upgrade, worth more than a feat. For a fighter it does nothing, because they already have all three.
- **#5 and #7** each give a **full feat plus gear**. That's the value of a variant human's bonus feat, handed out on a d20.
- **#12** gives a quasit familiar, normally a warlock's Pact of the Chain feature. The indefinite madness it comes with is a real cost, but only if the DM enforces it.
- **The costs are inconsistent.** Three results (#10, #12, #13) carry a drawback. The other seventeen don't.

### 4.4 Fateful moments contradict the rest of the chronicle

The d20 events were written without checking against the earlier steps:

- **#1 (parents murdered)** can contradict the Family step, where you may have rolled 2 living parents and a loving relationship with one.
- **#19 and #20** assume a parent with a mysterious book, or a secret noble family in Rexxentrum. They don't account for where you're actually from or who your family is.
- **#2** names a specific drow ("Il'viranya") with no follow-up guidance. It's a hook with nothing attached.

The book's fix is "reroll or adapt it with the DM". That works, but it means the table often can't be used as written.

### 4.5 Identities are stat blocks, not people

The Ally and Rival Identities table answers *"how strong is this NPC?"* and nothing else. It doesn't say what they do, what they want or where they are. That leads to:

- **Odd results.** Your loyal ally who owes you their life is a **Cultist**. Your nuisance rival who wants to be your friend is an **adult red dragon**.
- **The same table for allies and rivals.** An Archmage ally (1%) at level 1 can solve the party's problems. An Archmage rival can end the campaign.
- **No tie to the relationship.** The relationship roll and the identity roll don't affect each other, so the DM has to invent the link every time.

### 4.6 Prophecy rewards don't match the goals

Prophecy is the best-designed part of the chapter, but its rewards are the weakest:

| Reward | Problem |
|---|---|
| Inspiration after each long rest for 1d10 days | Good, but measured in **days**. In a dungeon crawl that's one session. On a sea voyage it may pass without a fight. |
| Advantage on saves vs frightened for 1d10 days | Very situational. Often does nothing. |
| +1d6 weapon damage for 1d4 days | Useless for most spellcasters, and lasts 1–4 days. |

And:

- **The rewards don't scale.** Finishing your arc-ending goal (the climax of the character's whole story) earns the same reward as the immediate goal.
- **It's all temporary.** A fateful moment from a lucky die roll gives you a *permanent* feat. Completing a goal you spent half the campaign on gives you *days* of a minor buff. The system rewards luck more than achievement.
- **"Takes on a prophetic tone"** is never explained. There's no guidance on how a completed goal changes the world or the character beyond the small buff.

### 4.7 Gaps and edge cases

Our reference doc lists these in full. The ones that affect play most:

| Gap | Effect at the table |
|---|---|
| **No class step** | The intro says you start from a blank slate, but class (the biggest choice) is never addressed. Nothing links class to background or fateful moments. |
| **No Revelry or Xarzith Kitril column** in Social Status | Pirate and Xarzith Kitril characters have no rule. The DM has to improvise. |
| **Family tables cover only villages and cities** | Towns, military outposts and the nomadic Velvin Thicket aren't covered. |
| **The d3 family relationships can exceed your family size** | Rolling 0 parents, 0 siblings and then 3 family relationships has no answer. |
| **Fateful moments #1 and #3 roll on Identities** | It isn't stated whether a ★ result there earns *another* fateful moment, so the rules may chain. |

### 4.8 Concentration and sameness

Settlement odds are weighted towards the capitals: about **22% of all rolled characters come from Rexxentrum**, and Rosohna, Zadash and Port Damali each get another 9–12%. In a party of five, two characters sharing a home city is common. The tables reflect population realistically, but that works against character variety.

On the family side, every family relationship is a **50/50 ally or rival**, and a typical character has about two of them. So most characters have at least one family member who's an active rival. That's dramatic, but a whole party of feuding families gets repetitive.

### 4.9 Time cost

A fully rolled chronicle takes about **13–20 dice rolls across 12+ tables**, spread over many pages. Each result also needs a moment of interpretation. That's fine for a dedicated session zero, but heavy if players are making characters at home or replacing a dead PC mid-campaign. The book has no "quick chronicle" option.

## 6. The numbers

**Assumptions:** every step rolled; Revelry characters use the Clovis Concord column and Xarzith Kitril characters use the Kryn Dynasty column (the book gives neither its own); the Acolyte's Dwendalian "ally or rival" is treated as an ally; an ally who rolls "loyal pet" (01–10) skips the identity roll and so can't trigger a fateful moment.

### Homeland → nation

| Nation column | Share |
|---|---|
| Dwendalian Empire (Marrow Valley + Zemni Fields) | 51% |
| Kryn Dynasty (Xhorhas) | 23% |
| Clovis Concord (Menagerie Coast) | 21% |
| Greying Wildlands | 5% |

### Acquired allies and rivals

| Count | Share |
|---|---|
| 0 | 19% |
| 1 | 69% |
| 2 | 12% |

Average **0.93** per character. Adding family (d3, average 2) gives about **2.9 named relationships per PC**.

### Fateful moments

- Chance a single acquired relationship triggers one: **27%** for an ally (30% × 90%, because of the pet result), **30%** for a rival.
- Per character: **74.5%** none, **24.5%** one, **1%** two. Average **0.27**.
- Party of 4: mixed outcome **69%**. Party of 5: **77%**.

### Settlements

Share of *all* rolled characters: Rexxentrum **22%**, Rosohna **12%**, Zadash **9%**, Port Damali **9%**.

## 7. What this means for the rework

These are design directions drawn from the analysis, not final decisions.

### Keep and build on

1. **Prophecy** as the core: three timescales, a complication for each, and the pacing formula.
2. **Mysterious Secrets** and their "concrete, unexplained, DM-owned" pattern.
3. **Relationships with behaviour rules**, and the full range of rival intensity.
4. **Social standing that depends on where you grew up**, delivered through the character, not through lore.
5. **Roll or choose at every step**, plus hidden DM rolls.

### Fix

1. **Give every character the same mechanical budget.** For example, every character gets exactly one formative boon, so the dice choose *which* boon, not *whether* you get one.
2. **Rebalance the boons into a single tier.** Remove or rework lycanthropy. Make sure bundles like #10 don't matter more to some classes than others. Either give every boon a cost or give none of them one.
3. **Tie the boon to the story.** Let it come from a relationship or event the player actually rolled (a mentor teaches you, a rival scarred you), not from a stat-block lottery.
4. **Make family count**, so a powerful family relationship can produce the same rewards as an acquired one.
5. **Give identities a role and a want**, not just a stat block, and roll power separately (or scale it to the campaign tier).
6. **Make Prophecy rewards scale and last.** Small for immediate goals, meaningful for long-term ones, permanent or signature for the arc-ending goal. Measure duration in sessions or uses, not in-game days.
7. **Fill the structural gaps.** Add a class step or class hooks, cover every nation and settlement type, handle families too small for their relationship count, and stop fateful moment rolls from chaining.
8. **Check every result against earlier steps**, or give each table explicit "if this conflicts…" fallbacks.
9. **Offer a quick mode**, a 5-roll version for mid-campaign replacement characters.

### Open questions

- Should the rework stay tied to Wildemount, or become **setting-neutral** with setting-specific modules?
- Is the goal **D&D 5e compatibility** (the 2014 or 2024 rules) or a system-agnostic backstory tool?
- How much should mechanical power come from backstory at all, compared with keeping it purely narrative and putting all rewards in Prophecy?
